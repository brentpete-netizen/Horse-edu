## Overview

The Clydesdale is Scotland’s native draught breed, developed in Lanarkshire from the mid-18th century. It is a powerful, stylish horse with characteristic white markings, silky feather, and a free walk and trot. Traditionally used for driving, farm work, and logging, Clydesdales are also increasingly ridden. The Clydesdale Horse Society (founded 1877) keeps the mother stud book; UK numbers remain limited (about 250 foals recorded per year).

## Key characteristics

Open forehead, flat profile, wide muzzle, intelligent eye; arched neck from an oblique shoulder with high withers. Strong back, deep spring of rib, long well-muscled quarters. Plentiful silky feather on strong, quality flat bone; free action at walk and trot. Typical white blaze and substantial white on feet and legs. Draught build; average weight about 1,600–1,800 lb (CHS breeding programme). Temperament described by CHS as very docile, gentle, and intelligent. Note: brown and roan coats are also cited by CHS but are not in the Notion color options.

## History

Native Scottish breed from Lanarkshire (“Clydesdale”). From the mid-18th century, local horses were improved with Flemish stallions (e.g. Duke of Hamilton’s import; Paterson of Lochlyloch). Recorded lines include the Lampits mare and Glancer (“Thompson’s Black Horse”), later Keir Peggy, Darnley, Baron’s Pride, and Baron of Buchlyvie. Peak export year 1911 (1,617 stallions); many served in WWI; mechanisation after WWII cut farm use. Clydesdale Horse Society founded 1877 (stud book from that year). Rare Breeds Survival Trust: “vulnerable” (1975), later “at risk.” Clydesdale Breeders of the U.S.A. incorporated 1879 (U.S. registry).
